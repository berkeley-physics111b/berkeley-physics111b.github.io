\section{Introduction}

Tomography as a general term refers to the process of reconstructing a 3D image using many 2D sections (or, more generally, reconstructing a higher dimensional image/state from a few `snapshots'/cross-sections). There are many contexts in which this idea is used ranging from medicine to oceanography to nuclear physics, and many ways in which it is done ranging from using x-rays to nuclear magnetic moments\footnote{Explore the NMR lab for more details.} to seismic waves. The MRI and the CT scan (historically the CAT scan) are maybe two of the most classic examples of tomography in practice.
%test comment

However, many methods of tomography require complex equipment, radiation that can be harmful in large doses, or both. And what if we are trying to image something large, like an active volcano,\footnote{Not a hypothetical example! Geologists want to use muon tomography to see how full magma chambers are, to potentially help predict eruptions.} or one of the Great Pyramids? The attenuation of most radiation sources is simply too significant to image such large structures.

In the 1960s, there was a burning question about the Great Pyramids of Giza. One of the pyramids, the Pyramid of Khafre, was not known to have any hidden chambers, and had far less in it then the Pyramids of Menkaure and Khufu. One would think that Khafre would wish to one-up his fellow pharaohs, but how to search for the hidden chambers? Physicist Luis Alvarez\footnote{Who also notably won the Nobel Prize for his discovery of resonant states using the bubble chamber, and with his son Walter Alvarez came up with the asteroid impact theory for dinosaur extinction.} suggested using muons to probe the pyramids: set detectors up at the base, look at the count rate in all directions, compare with predictions based on if the pyramid had no hidden chambers vs. if it did, and come to a conclusion - effectively, as he put it, ``x-ray'' the pyramids. He took data through the 1960s and concluded there were no hidden chambers. A group using the same approach found a hidden chamber in Khufu's pyramid in 2023! In this lab, we will use the same strategy to image the august and myriad hidden chambers of Evans Hall.

\subsection{Muons and cosmic rays}

Why is the muon such a good choice for imaging structures? First, they have a very high abundance - a couple muons have passed right through you in the time it took you to read this sentence. Second, they have high penetrating power, due to the combination of their relativistic lifetime and small stopping power. Together, these mean that they are ubiquitous for detection, and can penetrate most structures that we could be interested in imaging.

\subsubsection{Muon details}
Muons are particles that have a mass over 200 times that of the electron. Muon decay is of great importance in the study of weak interactions, one of the fundamental forces in nature. The value of the muon lifetime is one of the most precisely known constants, and is currently measured with a precision of a few parts per million. Low-energy muons are primarily created from decays of particles produced in the interactions of high-energy cosmic-rays with the earth's atmosphere. Eighty percent of the cosmic rays at sea level are muons, positively or negatively charged. They have a mean lifetime of a few microseconds.

They decay by the following processes:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Decay1}
    \mu^-\rightarrow e^-+\bar{\nu}_e+\nu_\mu
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Decay2}
    \mu^+\rightarrow e^++\nu_e+\bar{\nu}_\mu
\end{equation}
By the CPT theorem, the lifetimes of the negative and positive muons in \emph{vacuum} (i.e. due to reactions \eqref{eq:Decay1} and \eqref{eq:Decay2}) are equal. However, in matter, negative muons, when brought to rest in the close neighborhood of some nucleus (Z,A) a muonic atom is formed with the muon orbiting in the K-shell. The negative muon in the muonic atom can either decay as shown in reaction \eqref{eq:Decay2}, or it can disappear through the competing reaction:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Reaction3}
    \mu^- + p \rightarrow \nu_{\mu}+n
\end{equation}
where $p$ and $n$ are the proton and neutron respectively.
The rate of the muon-capture process \eqref{eq:Reaction3} depends strongly on the charge Z of the nucleus which captures the muon. In muonic carbon atoms, the rate of the muon capture \eqref{eq:Reaction3} is about 10\% the decay rate in \eqref{eq:Decay2}.

Most of the muons that are incident on the detector pass straight through it. Only the lowest energy particles come to rest within the scintillating liquid itself.

\subsubsection{East-west effect}

Due to the Earth's geomagnetic field, there is a preferred trajectory for incoming cosmic rays to our detector (see Figure \ref{fig:eastwest}). This asymmetry can be described as

\begin{equation}
    A = \frac{\Phi_W - \Phi_E}{\Phi_W + \Phi_E}
\end{equation}

This is also dependent on the zenith angle (angle of the Sun).

\subsection{Image reconstruction}
When we collect our data, we will obtain projections - effectively, the result of several line integrals through the object we are imaging - and attempt to construct a 3D (or 2D) image from them. (Roughly, a 2D image reconstruction is radiography, while a 3D image reconstruction is tomography - think of an x-ray vs. an MRI. We will not dwell extensively on the distinction here.) The exact algorithm you use will be one you discuss with staff as this lab is being finalized, but likely it will rely on the Fourier Slice Theorem, and will likely be the filtered backprojection algorithm - see Chapter 3 of Kak \& Slaney. %add details/confirm

\subsubsection{View signals}

The View Signals tab of the Muon Detection Program has several main settings, many of which carry over to the Live Histogram tab. There are three main categories of setting: channel-specific (both for channel 0 and channel 1), signal processing, and acquisition. Working from the bottom up on the side bar, the acquisition settings/controls are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sample rate (Hz)} - leave at 100 MHz - the sampling rate of the AD3 scope.
    \item \textbf{HW trigger channel (0 or 1)} - select which channel of the AD3 to trigger on.
    \item \textbf{Connect/disconnect} - Connect and disconnect to the AD3. When moving between tabs, you'll need to disconnect on one tab and then reconnect on the other.
    \item \textbf{Start/stop/clear} - Start, stop, and reset a measurement.
\end{itemize}

This also has a small status panel indicating if there is an active measurement, any errors in connecting, etc. The signal processing settings are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low pass cutoff (Hz)} - the program passes a received signal through a low pass filter in order to reduce noise. This sets the high-frequency cutoff for that filter.
    \item \textbf{Window start after trig ($\mu$s)} - Once the trigger is activated, we wish to ignore any pulses that occur shortly after, since they may simply be noise or part of the first pulse, as opposed to a muon decay event. This control allows the user to define how long this ignore period should last. This control should be large enough to ignore this noise, but not so large as to ignore a significant portion of the actual muon decay events.
    \item \textbf{Window stop after trig ($\mu$s) }- determines the length of the record (counting from the trigger), i.e. the maximum difference between the trigger and the second pulse. The triggering event is set to be at 1 $\mu$s of the way through each record, so that the digitizer can see pulses just before the trigger and so that some of the extra pretrigger data the AD3 automatically collects is discarded and doesn't affect processing speed.\footnote{There are some downsides to this - there were a lot of choices made in the code about how to minimize deadtime and how to quickly process incoming records. Something worth thinking about and discussing with staff if you have questions!} After this time has passed, the program analyzes the collected data for pulses, and then returns to listening for the next trigger event. This control should be just large enough to encompass a large majority of decay events.\footnote{If it is longer, this increases deadtime.}
    \item \textbf{Holdoff ($\mu$s)} - The amount of time after a pulse/trigger to wait before looking to see if there is another trigger. If this is zero, the same pulse may count as multiple trigger events.
    \item \textbf{Max traces to display} - how many traces to view at a given time.
\end{itemize}

The channel specific settings have a panel for both channel 0 and channel 1, with a checkbox to enable channel 1. Think carefully about what you expect to be the same or different across the channels! Also recall that the hardware itself can only trigger on one channel, so only one of the `first triggers' is actually a \textit{hardware} trigger and the other is a \textit{software} trigger (what does this mean, and how does it impact the experiment?). The channel specific settings are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First trigger level (V)} - the signal amplitude required for the trigger to be activated (looking for the first pulse).
    \item \textbf{Second trigger level (V) }- upon passing the first trigger level, the record is searched to see if a second pulse within the window defined in the signal processing settings exists which has an amplitude greater than this threshold (looking for the second decay pulse).
    \item \textbf{Expected pulses (1 or 2)} - Once the program has identified all of the peaks existing on the most recently received signal, it must determine whether or not these pulses represent a muon decay event. The event is registered as a true muon decay event only if the number of pulses on Channel 0 and on Channel 1 match exactly with the specified \textbf{expected pulses}. 
    There are four suggested use cases: [CH0 = 2, CH1 = 0]: This signifies a muon which entered the detector connected to channel 0, and then decayed in that same detector at a time at least as long as defined by \textbf{Pulse 2 delay}, but no longer than \textbf{Range}. Since there are only two pulses on this channel and no pulses on the other channel, we are convinced that another muon did not interfere with the measurement. For this setting, the user should set the trigger on Channel 0. [CH0 = 0, CH1 = 2]: Same as above, only for the detector connected to channel 1. For this case, the user should set the trigger on Channel 1. [CH0 = 1, CH1 = 2]: This represents a muon event in which a muon is first detected in one detector, but then it is detected in and decays in another detector, and no other pulses are recorded. It is suggested that for this case, the HW trigger channel is set to the channel which is connected to the detector which is on top, since more likely than not, we expect the muon to come from the above the surface of the Earth, and not below. [CH0 = 2, CH1 = 2]: This is a way to do a muon lifetime measurement in both channels, where each detector is effectively treated completely separately.
    \item \textbf{Voltage range (V p-p)} - This sets the voltage range of the AD3, and in so doing, effectively sets its resolution. See the AD3 manual for details. This value should be just large enough to encompass the amplitude of any received pulse.
    \item \textbf{Attenuation} - In order for the trigger to operate successfully (rising edge is assumed) a positive signal with respect to ground is expected on both channels. However, the incoming signal from the experiment may be negative, which would crash the program. Set the attenuation to -1.0 to invert the channel's signal, or to 1.0 to not invert it. Use the oscilloscope to check the signal polarity if you are not sure.
\end{itemize}

There is also a status panel that indicates how many events have been observed (i.e., records that passed the first trigger level) and how many counts/`passing triggers' there are (i.e., pulses that passed both the first and second trigger level).

Both programs initiate a record acquisition by the digitizer and, once the voltage exceeds the trigger threshold, retrieve voltage waveform data for both channels, incrementing the \textbf{events read (first trig)} count in the status panel by one. The programs remove the voltage offset by averaging the voltages of the first 200 samples of the record, all of which should occur before the triggering event, and subtracting that voltage from all the voltage waveform data. The programs then search for up to two pulses below (for -1.0 attenuation) or above (for 1.0 attenuation) the \textbf{Channel 0(1) first trigger,} which you set. This threshold is relative to the offset, not ground. The first pulse will correspond to the muon entering the scintillating material within the tank and the second to its decay. The time difference between the first and second pulses has to be less than the \textbf{window stop after trig}, which you set, and greater than the \textbf{window start after trig,} which you set. The range is simply a measure of how long the program will wait between pulses before moving on to the next signal. The pulse 2 delay is there in order to ignore any noise generated after the first signal that may pass the trigger level. Typically 1 microsecond is enough breathing time. You can specify how many pulses in each channel is required for the data to be written to disk; in our case we are looking for two events (i.e. the incident and the decay). The \textbf{passing triggers} is incremented if exactly that number is found in each channel. Whether or not the output condition is satisfied, the process repeats with the next data record.

The \textbf{View Signals} tab also displays the product of the two pulses so that you can get a sense of what your coincidence threshold should be.

It is important to note that in the program \textbf{View Signals}, after clicking \textbf{Start Acquisition}, the program continues to acquire records from the amplifier and searches them for the specified pulses until you click \textbf{Stop Acquisition}. This program graphs the \textbf{Raw signals} acquired by the digitizer, the \textbf{Signal with offset removed} and the \textbf{Filtered signal} for each enabled channel.

\subsubsection{Live Histogram}

Once you have determined the optimal settings for the program controls using the \textbf{Signal Viewer} tab, the next portion of the lab deals with the\textbf{Muon Lifetime} tab which measures the pulse height and width in addition to time.

There are a few additional settings on top of the ones for the \textbf{View Signal} tab, described below:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Histogram bins} - Set the number of bins to use in the histogram. This is for viewing convenience as you are performing data collection; you should be exporting the csv of data and performing your own analysis/constructing your own histogram for your report.
    \item \textbf{Export csv} - At any point you can export a csv of all the data collected to that point. However, it's not strictly necessary if you set a peak-data log path (below) before beginning your run (strongly recommended).
    \item \textbf{Set peak-data log path} - This allows you to set a file name to save data to at the beginning. The program automatically logs data at periodic intervals so that even if the computer crashes, your data up to that point is not lost.
    \item \textbf{Save all charged particles} - For one of the investigation options - saves data for all records that pass the first trigger, whether or not they pass the second trigger (indicates whether or not the second trigger is passed for each record).
    \item \textbf{Save raw traces to csv} - For one of the investigation options - saves the raw trace/record data to a separate csv (the path you set above with `\_raw\_trace' appended), with the regular record statistics (pulse height, FWHM, time, etc) goint to the path you set above. Do \textit{not} select this for long runs without discussing with a staff member - you will end up with an entirely too large file and may crash the computer.
    \item \textbf{Rate (Hz)} - additional status indicator indicating the average count rate.
\end{itemize}

When you click start, the program begins searching for pulses according to the conditions you set, just like in the \textbf{Signal Viewer} tab. When an output condition is satisfied (i.e. exactly the specified number of pulses is found in each channel, up to the maximum of 2), the program writes out a record with the following values: timestamp, whether or not it passed both triggers (relevant for the `save all charged particles' option), time, amplitude, and width of four pulses: 1st pulse in Channel 0, 2nd pulse in Channel 0, 1st pulse in Channel 1, 2nd pulse in Channel 1, time between pulses in Channel 0, time between pulses in Channel 1, and time between first pulse across channels. \subsubsection{Muon Tomography}

The key distinction between the software for this lab and the muon lifetime lab (MUO) is that whereas in MUO you are largely considering channels separately, in this lab you are focusing on whether the channels see a pulse at the same time (coincidence) and if so, whether the muon decays in one of the channels (stoppage). Because there are limitations on the timing resolution of the AD3 and the muons are moving fast enough that it is hard to resolve the time between them passing through the first and second detector, we use a trick: we take the product of the two pulses. As you'll see in the prelab, the amplitude of the resulting pulse is dependent on $dy = y_1 - y_2$, where $y_1,y_2$ are the heights of the two pulses, and $dt = t_1 - t_2$, where $t_1, t_2$ are the arrival times of the two pulses. By figuring out the distribution of $dy$ and setting a minimum amplitude for the product pulse (referred to here on out as the `coincidence threshold' - note that this is a \textit{software} trigger, not a hardware trigger!) we then bound our $dt$ between the two pulses. To search for a stoppage, we search for a coincidence and then look for a decay pulse as normal - but we only look for the decay pulse in the second detector (the muon can't decay twice!). Thus, when we refer to the `stoppage threshold', we really mean the second trigger threshold on the second channel.

The muon tomography tab has a slightly different subset of settings, though they are all related to the settings you have adjusted before. Going through the sidebar, the top section for hardware settings includes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HW trigger channel (0 or 1)} - select which channel of the AD3 to trigger on.
    \item \textbf{Stoppage channel (0 or 1)} - which channel you are looking for stoppages in - generally should be `Bra', the larger detector (why?).
    \item \textbf{HW trigger level (V)} - this is effectively the first trigger level, which controls whether the software looks for a coincidence/stoppage at all.
    \item \textbf{Sample rate (Hz)}, \textbf{Voltage range (V p-p)}, \textbf{Attenuation} - As above.
\end{itemize}

The signal processing settings are all as in the other tabs. The main differences are in the software trigger section, where you set the:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coincidence threshold (V$^2$)} - the minimum threshold for the product of the two pulses to consider the pulses as a coincidence. You should decide this based on your prelab and the muon lifetime data you take.
    \item \textbf{Stoppage threshold (V)} - the minimum threshold for the height of the second decay pulse in one of the channels. This is the same as the second trigger level above, just only checked in the stoppage channel you select. This is what checks whether a coincidence pulse is also a stoppage.
    \item \textbf{Save (stoppages only, all data)} - select whether you want to save only stoppages or all data (coincidences and stoppages). The produced .csv file will indicate whether a recorded pulse was a coincidence or stoppage.
\end{itemize}

In the data saving section, everything is the same as in the other tabs except for the \textbf{Integration time (min)}, which is used to calculate the average coincidence and stoppage rates on display and set the bin size for the count rate histogram.

The tab displays the average coincidence and stoppage rates, the rates over the time of the run, the total number of coincidences and stoppages, and one of the recent acquired traces (as a sanity check for your settings as you begin the run). You should recalculate/replot most of these values yourselves; the .csv file that is saved will simply contain a record of the coincidences/stoppages seen and data about these pulses.
